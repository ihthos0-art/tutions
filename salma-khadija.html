Schools Should Start Later

Every morning, millions of students drag themselves out of bed before the sun has fully risen. They sit in classrooms, half-asleep, expected to focus and learn. But research shows that teenagers need between eight and ten hours of sleep each night — and most are not getting anywhere close to that. Forcing students to arrive at school before 8:00 a.m. means forcing them to wake up before their bodies are biologically ready.

Studies by the American Academy of Pediatrics show that when schools shift their start times to 8:30 a.m. or later, students score higher on tests, report lower levels of stress, and miss fewer days of school. Sleep is not a luxury — it is a necessity. A school that ignores this science is setting its students up to fail before the first bell even rings.

Phones Do Not Belong in School

Walk into any classroom and you will likely find at least one student sneaking a glance at a phone under the desk. Every buzz, every notification pulls attention away from the lesson. Research consistently shows that students who use their phones during class retain less information and score lower on assessments than those who put their devices away entirely.

Some argue that phones can be useful learning tools. But schools already have computers, tablets, and libraries for that purpose. The real competition is not between phones and pencils — it is between learning and social media, games, and group chats. Teenagers, whose brains are still developing impulse control, are the least equipped to resist that pull. Schools have a responsibility to protect the learning environment, and that means keeping phones out of it.

Community Gardens

In the middle of a busy city, it is easy to feel disconnected from nature. Tall buildings block the sun. Concrete covers the ground. Traffic noise replaces birdsong. But in one neighborhood in Brooklyn, a group of residents decided to change that. They turned an empty lot on the corner of Washington Avenue into a community garden.

At first, only three families helped. They pulled weeds, spread soil, and planted tomato seeds. Word spread quickly. Within a year, twenty families had plots. Neighbors who had never spoken before were sharing tools, advice, and extra vegetables. Children who used to play video games indoors were now digging in the dirt and learning where food actually comes from.

The garden did not just grow plants. It grew relationships. A retired teacher started a free reading program under the shade of the oak tree. A local chef taught cooking classes using vegetables from the garden. The city noticed the transformation and began supporting similar projects in other neighborhoods. What started as a small idea became proof that a little green space can change an entire community.

Question 1 — Central Idea

What is the most important idea in this passage? Write it in one sentence.

Question 2 — Supporting Detail

Find one detail from the passage that shows the garden helped the community. Use words from the text.

Question 3 — Make a Connection

Does your neighborhood have a park, garden, or community space? Write one sentence about why it is important.
