Libraries Are More Important Than Ever

Some people believe that libraries have become unnecessary now that information is available online at any moment. But this argument ignores what libraries actually provide. A library is not just a collection of books — it is a free, open space where anyone, regardless of income, can access computers, read newspapers, attend programs, and find help from trained librarians. For millions of people who cannot afford home internet, the library is the only connection they have to the wider world.

Studies show that children who regularly visit libraries perform better in school and develop stronger reading habits than those who do not. Librarians are also experts at helping people evaluate sources and identify misinformation — a skill more critical now than ever. Cutting library funding to save money is a false economy: the communities that lose their libraries pay a far greater price in educational decline and lost opportunity. Libraries are not relics. They are essential public services.

Students Should Have a Say in School Rules

Every day, students follow rules they had no part in making. They sit where they are told, eat when a bell rings, and are expected to obey policies written entirely by adults. Yet students are the ones most affected by those policies. When young people have no voice in the rules that govern them, they are more likely to disengage, resist, or feel that school is something done to them rather than for them.

Schools that have created student advisory boards or class councils report a shift in school culture. When students help shape a rule, they are more likely to follow it and help others follow it too. Research in education shows that students who feel ownership over their environment are more motivated, more cooperative, and more likely to stay enrolled. Giving students a real voice in school decisions is not a loss of authority for adults — it is an investment in responsibility, leadership, and trust.

The Borrowed Book

Leo had worked at the Maple Street branch library for thirty-one years, and in that time the world had quietly decided it no longer needed him. The shelves were dusty, the reading chairs sagged, and most days the only sound was the radiator ticking and the rain scratching at the windows. People passed the building without looking up; they could carry a thousand books in their pockets now. Leo kept the lights on anyway. He shelved returns that hadn’t come, ordered books no one would read, and each morning set out a little sign that read, in his careful handwriting: Free. Warm. Open to all.

One rainy Thursday, the door banged open and a girl stumbled in — maybe sixteen, soaked through, a wet backpack clinging to her shoulders. Her name was Jade. She needed a book on architecture, she said — not for a class but for a scholarship portfolio due in six months. She wanted to design buildings that could fit in crowded, broken neighborhoods like hers. Leo felt the old ache: the architecture shelf held exactly four books, all of them outdated, none of them what she needed. He could see her shoulders fall before he said a word.

But on his way to apologize, Leo paused. For twenty years he had filled a sketchbook of his own — buildings he wished the city would build, drawn in ink during the quiet hours: a school with a rooftop garden, a clinic shaped like an open hand, a library that looked like a lantern. It was the only valuable thing he owned, and he had never shown it to anyone. He set it on the desk between them. “It’s not a real book,” he said. “But it’s all I have. Take it for the week.”

Jade took it, and the next Thursday she came back — not to return it but to show him a page of her own sketches, rough but alive, drawn in the margins beside his. Then the next week, and the next. Through the winter the two of them spread their drawings across the reading table while the radiator ticked and the rain came down. He taught her how a doorway could welcome; she taught him that a building could bend. The empty library, for a few hours each week, became the most crowded room on the street.

Six months later an envelope arrived from a scholarship board. Jade had won. They invited Leo to the ceremony, and he sat in the back in his only good shirt while she walked to the stage. “I want to thank,” she said into the microphone, “the librarian who had no book to give me, but gave me his only one.” He felt the whole room turn to look at him, and for the first time in years he wanted to be seen.

Walking home that night through the rain, Leo understood that the library had never been empty. For thirty-one years he had been pouring his only valuable thing — his attention, his drawings, his belief that someone might walk in — into a room the city had written off as useless. He had looked down at the shelves and seen scarcity. But the value had been him all along, waiting for someone to share it with. Generosity, he realized, creates value where scarcity seems to exist.

Central Idea

Generosity creates value where scarcity seems to exist.
