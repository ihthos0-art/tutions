Libraries Are More Important Than Ever

Some people believe that libraries have become unnecessary now that information is available online at any moment. But this argument ignores what libraries actually provide. A library is not just a collection of books — it is a free, open space where anyone, regardless of income, can access computers, read newspapers, attend programs, and find help from trained librarians. For millions of people who cannot afford home internet, the library is the only connection they have to the wider world.

Studies show that children who regularly visit libraries perform better in school and develop stronger reading habits than those who do not. Librarians are also experts at helping people evaluate sources and identify misinformation — a skill more critical now than ever. Cutting library funding to save money is a false economy: the communities that lose their libraries pay a far greater price in educational decline and lost opportunity. Libraries are not relics. They are essential public services.

Students Should Have a Say in School Rules

Every day, students follow rules they had no part in making. They sit where they are told, eat when a bell rings, and are expected to obey policies written entirely by adults. Yet students are the ones most affected by those policies. When young people have no voice in the rules that govern them, they are more likely to disengage, resist, or feel that school is something done to them rather than for them.

Schools that have created student advisory boards or class councils report a shift in school culture. When students help shape a rule, they are more likely to follow it and help others follow it too. Research in education shows that students who feel ownership over their environment are more motivated, more cooperative, and more likely to stay enrolled. Giving students a real voice in school decisions is not a loss of authority for adults — it is an investment in responsibility, leadership, and trust.

The River Road Cleanup

For years, the creek running through Riverside Park had been full of life. Children caught fireflies near its banks in summer, and small fish could be seen just below the surface. But when a factory opened nearby, waste began seeping into the water. Within months, the fish disappeared, the water turned brown, and a sharp smell kept families away from the park entirely.

A group of fifth graders noticed the change during a class field trip. Their science teacher, Mr. Osei, helped them understand that the factory’s runoff was the cause. Determined to act, the students met after school to form a plan. They collected water samples, wrote letters to the town council, and organized a weekend cleanup of trash along the creek banks. Their evidence was difficult to ignore.

Because of the students’ effort, the town council fined the factory and required it to install a water filtration system. Over the following months, the water slowly cleared. By the next fall, small fish reappeared near the shallow bend. Families returned to the park. The students learned an important lesson: when you identify a cause and are willing to act on it, you have the power to change the outcome.
